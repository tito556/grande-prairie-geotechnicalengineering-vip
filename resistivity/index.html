A contractor called us last spring about a proposed warehouse off 116 Avenue. The borehole logs showed saturated clays and silt till down to 18 meters, but they needed a continuous profile across the full site to map buried channels before footing design. That is exactly the scenario where we run a Vertical Electrical Sounding grid. Grande Prairie sits on thick Quaternary deposits—glacial till, glaciolacustrine clays, and outwash sands—and resistivity contrasts between these units are sharp enough to trace stratigraphy without opening the ground. We combine VES with CPT soundings when the project demands both mechanical and electrical stratigraphy, particularly near the Bear Creek corridor where sand lenses complicate excavation planning.

Resistivity contrasts in Grande Prairie's glacial sequence often exceed 10:1 between clean outwash gravels and underlying clay till—ideal targets for VES mapping.

Method and coverage

Grande Prairie grew fast after the 1950s oil and gas boom, and much of the early infrastructure was placed on native till with limited subsurface data. Today's redevelopments and expansions north of 100 Avenue encounter the same variable overburden, but now we can resolve it with surface geophysics before breaking ground. In our lab, the resistivity workflow follows ASTM D6431 for 2D profiling and VES interpretation, using a Wenner-Schlumberger array that reaches investigation depths of 30 to 50 meters. The raw apparent resistivity curves are inverted with smooth-model least-squares routines, and we calibrate the geoelectric layers against borehole logs or test pits whenever available. A typical VES sounding in the County of Grande Prairie takes 45 to 70 minutes per station, depending on electrode spacing and contact resistance in the dry summer surface crust.

Regional considerations

The Smoky River and Bear Creek valleys cut through Grande Prairie's glacial package, leaving terraces where resistivity can flip from 800 Ω·m in dry gravel to under 10 Ω·m in clay-filled paleochannels within 20 horizontal meters. Skipping a resistivity survey in these transition zones means foundation designs based on a single borehole that missed a soft lens three meters away. We have seen sites where a high-resistivity surface layer masked a low-resistivity saturated silt at 4 meters depth, and the excavation plan had to be rewritten after VES profiling revealed the true geometry. Seasonal groundwater perched on the till surface also shifts resistivity seasonally—spring melt raises pore-water conductivity, and the same VES line run in August can show 15 to 25 percent higher values in the upper 3 meters. Our reports flag these seasonal effects explicitly.

Top questions

What depth can a VES survey reach in Grande Prairie's glacial sediments?

With a maximum Wenner electrode spacing of 40 meters, the effective investigation depth typically reaches 25 to 30 meters in the local till and outwash. We can extend to 50 meters using Schlumberger arrays with longer current-electrode offsets, though resolution at depth decreases as the signal averages over a larger volume. The practical limit is often set by site access and the available spread length rather than the method itself.

How much does an electrical resistivity survey cost for a typical lot in Grande Prairie?

For a standard residential or light commercial lot, a 1D VES survey with three to five sounding stations generally runs between CA$790 and CA$1,590, depending on access conditions and the number of depth steps. A 2D ERI profile of 120 to 200 linear meters usually falls within the same range. We provide a fixed-price quote after reviewing the site plan and project objectives.

Can resistivity surveys work when the ground is frozen?

Frozen ground poses a real challenge because ice is a strong electrical insulator—resistivity can jump by an order of magnitude in the upper 30 to 60 centimeters. We typically schedule Grande Prairie resistivity work between May and October. If winter work is unavoidable, we use salt-water-saturated bentonite at electrode contacts and apply a frozen-layer correction during inversion, though data quality in the top meter will still be reduced compared to summer readings.
